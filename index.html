Step 4 of 4 — Topic Bridge

One question to bring us into today's topic. Take your time. Pass is always fine.

"When you notice an emotion — do you usually evaluate it first (should I feel this? is this okay?) or describe it first (what is this, exactly)? Which happens faster for you?"

This question lands the distinction before the psychoeducation does. Most people will recognize the evaluating pattern — "I shouldn't feel this way" or "this is bad" happens almost instantly. The point isn't to judge that habit; it's to make it visible so the group has something concrete to work with in the teaching section.

◈
Describe vs. Evaluate

Most of us were not taught to describe our emotions. We were taught to manage them. The difference is more significant than it sounds.

The core distinction: Describing means noticing and naming what's actually happening — like a scientist observing data. Evaluating means adding a verdict about whether what's happening should be happening. Most of the suffering that comes from emotions is not from the emotion itself — it's from what we tell ourselves about it.
△ Evaluating
  • I shouldn't feel this way
  • This is bad / wrong / too much
  • Why am I always like this?
  • I need to get rid of this feeling
  • This means something is wrong with me
◆ Describing
  • I notice something that feels like sadness
  • There's tightness in my chest right now
  • I'm feeling more activated than usual today
  • Something happened and anger arose
  • I notice a pulling-away feeling
This distinction is easy to understand intellectually and very difficult in practice. Don't rush past the examples — read them aloud if it helps. The evaluating column will often produce recognition in the room. You can pause and ask "do any of these sound familiar?" before moving on.

◉
Observe — Describe — Participate

These are the three core mindfulness "What Skills" in DBT. Together, they form a sequence for engaging with inner experience without being consumed by it or running from it.

I
Observe

Notice what's happening without immediately reacting, labeling, or judging. Just attend to the experience itself. Step back and watch.

II
Describe

Put words to what you observe — the facts, the sensations, the quality of the experience. Stick to what you can directly know. No interpretation yet.

III
Participate

Once you've observed and described, you can engage more fully — from a place of clarity rather than reactivity. Throw yourself in without losing your footing.

Why This Sequence Matters

Most emotional suffering happens when we skip Observe and Describe and go straight to Participate — but from inside a story about the emotion rather than the emotion itself. The sequence creates a small gap between stimulus and response. That gap is where choice lives.

Secondary Emotions — The Emotion About the Emotion

One of the most important concepts in describing emotions without judgment is understanding secondary suffering — what we add to an emotion by evaluating it.

The evaluating path:
Sadness arises
→
"I shouldn't feel sad"
→
Shame about sadness
◆     ◆
The describing path:
Sadness arises
→
"I notice sadness"
→
Just sadness — no second layer
The point is not to eliminate feelings. Sadness will still be there in the second path. What changes is that you're not also dealing with shame, self-judgment, or the exhausting work of fighting what's already here. The emotion itself is often much more manageable than the emotion plus the verdict.
Secondary emotions are a significant source of suffering for most people in DBT groups. Common examples worth naming explicitly: anger about feeling anxious, shame about anger, fear about feeling numb, self-criticism about feeling sad. These second-layer emotions are often more activating than the original. You can ask the group: "Does anyone recognize this — feeling something, and then feeling bad about feeling it?"

◆
The Language of Describing

The way we phrase emotion descriptions matters. "I am sad" fuses the self with the emotion. "I notice sadness" creates a small but meaningful distance — an observer perspective.

Fused Language

  • I am anxious
  • I'm a mess
  • I'm depressed
  • I'm so angry
  • I'm broken

Observing Language

  • I notice anxiety arising
  • There's a lot happening right now
  • I'm experiencing something heavy today
  • Anger is present for me right now
  • Something feels fragmented today

Useful Describing Stems

  • "I notice..."
  • "There's a feeling of..."
  • "Something that feels like..."
  • "Right now I'm experiencing..."
  • "I'm aware of..."
  • "In my body there's..."

◇   What about body sensations?

+

Emotions live in the body before they live in language. Describing body sensations is often easier and more precise than naming an emotion — and it bypasses the judgment reflex, because "tightness in my chest" is harder to evaluate than "anxiety."

Useful body description vocabulary: tightness, heaviness, warmth, coolness, pressure, fluttering, expansion, constriction, buzzing, stillness, holding, trembling, emptiness, fullness, weight, lightness.

◇   What if I don't know what I'm feeling?

+

"I don't know" is a perfectly valid description. So is "something" or "a lot." Many people — especially those with trauma histories or alexithymia — have difficulty identifying emotions with precision. Starting with body sensations first is often more accessible. "I notice tension in my shoulders and a tightness in my throat" is more information than it might seem.

Large Group Discussion 12–15 min

◇
These are invitations. One question explored well is more valuable than five covered quickly.

Low-Demand Opener

Were there any words in that vocabulary list that surprised you — something that put language to something you didn't have a word for?

The Evaluation Habit

What's a feeling you notice you tend to evaluate before you've even finished experiencing it — something that gets a verdict almost instantly?

Secondary Emotions

Has anyone had the experience of feeling worse not because of the original emotion, but because of what you told yourself about it? What does that cycle look like for you?

The Language Shift

What's the difference — for anyone who's tried it — between saying "I am anxious" and "I notice anxiety"? Does the phrasing change anything for you?

Deeper

Where did you learn to evaluate your emotions rather than describe them? Were feelings in your family growing up treated as problems to be solved, or as something worth just noticing?

Sequence: Start with the vocabulary opener — it's concrete, low-demand, and usually generates real engagement. The evaluation habit question tends to produce strong recognition. The secondary emotions question can bring up grief and relief simultaneously — some people have never had language for that cycle. The "where did you learn this" question is the deepest and most optional — use it if the group has energy and time; skip if you're running long.

Watch for: People who describe their emotions very diagnostically ("I have depression, so I feel...") — gently invite them back to what's actually happening in the body right now. Also watch for the group using the describing language somewhat mechanically at first; that's fine. It takes practice for it to feel real.

Things Worth Carrying Out

◆
Describing is not the same as approving. You can notice anger without deciding anger is correct. You can observe sadness without deciding it's acceptable. The description is neutral — the emotion is not on trial.
◆
Most emotional suffering has two layers: the original feeling, and the verdict you add to it. You can only change the second layer. The first one is just what's here.
◆
You do not have to fix what you name. Naming an emotion clearly and precisely is itself a form of regulation — not because it makes it go away, but because it makes it less overwhelming.
◆
The gap between stimulus and response is where choice lives. Observe and describe are how you create that gap.
◆
Not knowing what you're feeling is also data. "Something is here and I can't name it yet" is a valid and honest description.
